## What this app is

- **A seating chart, attendance and points tool that runs *alongside* Gynzy / NaniBox / Classroom Screen** — not a replacement for them. Gynzy already does whiteboard, lessons, timers and noise meters well; its seating chart and attendance are weak. That gap is the product.
- **Target user: teachers who want marble-jar dynamics but can't have the jar.** Derek's room runs on a physical marble jar, a per-class jar, and a battery wall that fills toward a treasure chest. Colleagues like watching it work and default to ClassDojo instead because they can't replicate it.
- **Deliberately fewer features than ClassDojo**: no parent accounts, no behaviour taxonomy, no setup. The things people complain about are the things we leave out. That's a position, not a limitation.
- **The name is Class? Yes!**, the call-and-response every teacher already uses to get the room: the teacher calls, the whole class answers. It is a whole-class moment, which is what the app is for. Written ClassYes wherever punctuation can't go (web address, app stores, search); classyes.app is the address to buy, and classyes.com is a premium domain worth buying only once teachers are paying. Names with "jar" were ruled out because ClassJar, Jar of Stars and Classroom Screen's behaviour jar already exist; Class Go, Class Ready, Ready Class and Ready to Learn all clash with products or programmes that exist. The phrase is too common to trademark on its own.
- **The logo is drawn in code, not a picture.** Two chalk speech bubbles, "Class?" in white and "Yes!" answering in yellow (`ClassYesLogo.tsx`), redrawn from a Gemini sketch. As an SVG it stays sharp on any board, and the words are live Andika, so the `a` is the one the students write. It is the splash screen's headline, with "Welcome, Teacher!" moved under it as a smaller line. The 2+2=4 doodle moved left to make room, because the taller headline pushed the button down on top of it. It moved again when Sign in with Google widened the row under the classes: it is up the left edge now, between the sun and the cat, and the chalk star sits between the dog and the house. **A splash taller than the screen is scaled down as a whole to fit**, rather than squeezed: five classes with Sign in on a 1024x640 laptop needed it, and five classes alone was already cut off top and bottom there. Screens it fits are untouched. **The bunting hangs over the top of the logo, on purpose** (2026-10-04): it was made a third shallower to clear the logo and put back the same day. The teacher likes it big, overlapping the logo the way real bunting hangs; shallow, it looked small and the board out of proportion. Don't shrink it again. **A doodle never sits on the words or a card**: they are placed by hand at the edges, but every new arrangement in the middle (one class or five, signed in or not, a short laptop or a big board) put one on top of something - the grapes on "...every computer", the cat and dog under the cards. The splash now hides any doodle that would touch the logo, a line of words, a class card or a button, measured before anything is painted and again when the screen or the fonts change.
- **The mascot is the splash screen's chalkboard, cheering**: wood frame, green board, a chalk smile with its eyes shut, both arms thrown up for the "Yes!" and a few stars. The artwork is the teacher's own, made in Gemini and traced to SVG in Canva. The whole character is the browser-tab icon (`public/favicon.svg`); the close-up tile (one arm, the frame's corner) is the home-screen icon (`apple-touch-icon.png`), on its own green because iOS fills see-through corners with black. A simpler redraw was made for tab size first and replaced by his: at 32px his reads fine, and at 16px any tab icon is a coloured square with a face. The speech bubbles stay as the name on the splash; the mascot is the face of the app where there is no room for words.
- Because it's designed to share the screen rather than own it, **coexisting with the lesson app is the core premise**, not a nice-to-have.
- **Don't race the widget makers** (2026-10-19, the teacher, from a Facebook post of Canva's new classroom tools: Name Picker, Countdown, Dice, Coin Flip, Clock, Now / Next, Participation Tracker, Classroom Noise Level, Learning Goals; Classroomscreen's spinner wheel is the same). AI now makes one-off widgets cheap, so anyone can have them and none of them sets an app apart. A widget forgets everything when it closes. What they can't copy quickly is what joins the tools up: **the children where they sit** (who is away, who hasn't had a turn), **memory over time** (the goal over a term, the participation record, the Line's steps, the Quest's semester), **craft for a real board** (the double touch, fitting any screen with no setting changed, smooth on a weak 4K board), and **privacy** (names in the teacher's own Google account, no parent messaging). So no dice, coin flip or clock, and no noise meter or lesson schedule (Deliberately not building). Canva's "Participation Tracker: award class points" is the one on this app's ground, a plain counter from the look of it; that teachers want it is why the Quest stays next. Canva is no rival for the lesson itself: a lesson presented from Canva is one more thing the floating class goal sits over. A line for the pitch: "Canva can pick a name. Class? Yes! knows where Kevin sits, whether he's here today, and that it's his turn."

- **Treasure Quest: keys, five chests and a prize wheel** (2026-10-07, from the teacher's lessons; in a preview, "Treasure Quest Preview", a Claude artifact; the plan agreed 2026-10-12; nothing built yet). **Why**: reaching the goal lands flat unless the teacher has a real reward ready - the chest on the board is empty. The app can't supply the reward, and shouldn't try (no hatching or decorated avatars: they reward individuals, invite comparison, are ClassDojo's ground, and kids tire of in-app prizes faster than real ones). What it can do is give the goal a longer arc, build the suspense, and make the teacher's real reward easy and visible. **The shape agreed in talk**:
  - **The plan, agreed** (2026-10-12, the teacher: "I am happy with this concept and plan"). It answers the open questions in the points below, and where it differs from them it wins. The Quest is the class goal's: student points and no points have none.
    - **They are Adventures in the app**, because "theme" already means the app's colours in Class Settings.
    - **The key moment is medium**: when the goal fills, a big key comes to the middle of the board over a light dim, with "You got a key!" and a short jingle, no GIF and no fanfare. It waits for a tap, which a student can make ("Who wants to put the key in?"), then flies into the chest at the meter's end; a click, and one lock turns gold.
    - **A chest with all its keys opens on a tap**, rattling at the meter's end until the teacher is ready, then or at the end of the lesson: that is the big moment, the chest filling the screen, bursting open, and the wheel rising out of it.
    - **The wheel**: eight slices; a tap spins it for about 6 seconds (Pick a Spinner, or the teacher's Spin), popping over each slice and crawling through the last two seconds so the class can cheer it on; only the slice it lands on turns over. **Three slice colours: plain for small prizes, silver for medium, gold for big**, so there is something better to root for on every wheel, even Wood's.
    - **The odds** (small, silver, gold): Wood 6, 2, 0; Bronze 4, 4, 0; Silver 2, 5, 1; Gold 0, 6, 2; **Mega all eight gold**. The first table gave the Mega chest an even chance of a medium prize, which after a whole term would land flat in front of thirty children, and the Gold chest could give a small one. Now every quest ends on a big prize (about 1.4 a quest on average), no chest's worst prize is worse than the usual one of the chest before, and the first gold slice, on the Silver wheel, is a moment of its own.
    - **Pace: a quest a semester**, 15 keys at about one a week, so two adventures a school year. Points tells the teacher "The last key took 6 days.", to help set the goal's size.
    - **The guardian holds the Mega chest's fifth key**, so it adds no time: the term's last goal is its fight. Through that goal the stars fly at the guardian instead of the coin; it wobbles and roars at each hit and its puff runs down. When the goal fills, **Showdown** starts on a tap: three to five picked students each tap to throw, a little luck in each, until it sits down out of puff, hands over the key and becomes the class's friend. **It never wins and never takes a star.** A worksheet "fed to the guardian" needs nothing new: it is a star on the student's desk, and the star is a hit.

## Settled mechanics

- **The app never scrolls.** Not the board, not a screen that slides over it, not the side panel. Everything is laid out to fit the viewport, and when there is more than fits, the layout adapts (fewer rows, smaller chips) rather than the page growing. A modal may scroll its *body*, but the modal itself always fits — it is capped at 90% of the viewport with a scrolling body, so its title and edges never leave the screen. This is a rule, not a preference: a teacher at a board never scrolls to find a control.

- **Screen sizes and devices: it fits any screen from 1024x500 up, with no setting changed** (2026-10-02, the teacher's rule: "it will be used on various smartboards and screens, and must need no tweaking of Windows or browser settings"). The teacher's own board is a 4K panel with Windows at 300%, which, after Chrome's tabs, bookmarks bar and the taskbar, gives the page **1280x559** - shorter than anything it had been checked at, and the side panel ran off the bottom: by 106px with the timer controls open, 72px with the class list open, 8px on the flip cards. Turning Chrome's zoom or Windows' scaling down would have fixed his board and no one else's, so the app fits itself instead. **Nothing opens by pushing the side panel down**: the class list opens over the panel like a menu (a tap anywhere else closes it), and the timer controls are a drawer that **slides out from under the clock** and over Pickers & Points, in the timer's colour, while the time is being set; a tap on the timer slides it back, as before. The drawer's window starts tucked under the clock's rounded bottom and clips what is in it, so the controls come out from beneath the clock; it is one CSS slide the graphics chip runs. The first version faded in over the clock for a moment and then popped down, and on the board it looked broken (2026-10-03). **The side panel tightens itself to its height** (`hooks/useFitToHeight.ts`): drawn, measured, and if it overflows, one step tighter, before anything is painted - the gaps first, then the buttons' padding, then the dial, then the Pick All row. Text never shrinks for it, and a screen it fitted is drawn exactly as before. - **Flip Cards: one tap, one meaning, nothing timed.** A student comes up and taps a card; whoever it reveals has the turn, and the side panel's +/- go to them - no selecting a card first, because a tap on a face-up card is how it gets put away. The newest card glows amber; earlier face-up cards dim but stay readable, and stay up. Tapping a faded card gives it the turn back (a spelling bee's "try again") and fades whoever had it. **Only the glowing card can be put away**, and a tap on it puts it on the discard pile. **Flip Back went** (2026-10-05, the teacher: "we don't need it"): a switch on the flip toolbar turned the glowing card face down again for another go in the same round, so the same tap meant two things depending on a switch. Every student gets a turn before anyone has a second, and Shuffle starts the next round. Timed flip-back and set-aside were built and taken out: the teacher, not a clock, decides when a turn ends. So discarding a faded card takes two deliberate taps - wake it, then put it away - which is the protection a double-tap-to-discard would have given, without asking for a gesture the smart board fakes on its own.   - **Status.** Groups are for projects as much as games, so every card carries one of four states: Working, Help, Ready, Done. Ready and Done are deliberately different — Ready means a group has stopped and is waiting for the teacher, Done means there is nothing left to do. **The status colours the card, it is not a widget on the card.** The body and the points row both take a **strong** wash in the status colour and the band and frame keep the group's own colour, so scanning the room is "look for the red card", which is the red, amber and green cups on a group's table. The wash is deliberately heavy: the first attempt used a 16% tint and from the back of a room it was indistinguishable from the next card. Name chips sit on the plain card colour so they stay crisp on top of it. The small status chip in the band wears the status colour too, ringed in the band's own text colour so it still separates when a red group is the one asking for help — a faded chip said nothing at a distance and the chip and the body should say the same thing. Working has no wash at all: if every card were tinted the board would go one flat colour and a single red card would stop standing out. Help pulses a soft ring, and it is the only status that moves, because it is the only one asking for you now. Icons are Pencil, question mark, thumbs up and tick, chosen to be read without English — a raised hand turns to mush at small sizes and a flag has to be taught.
