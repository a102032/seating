## What this app is

- **A seating chart, attendance and points tool that runs *alongside* Gynzy / NaniBox / Classroom Screen** — not a replacement for them. Gynzy already does whiteboard, lessons, timers and noise meters well; its seating chart and attendance are weak. That gap is the product.
- **Target user: teachers who want marble-jar dynamics but can't have the jar.** Derek's room runs on a physical marble jar, a per-class jar, and a battery wall that fills toward a treasure chest. Colleagues like watching it work and default to ClassDojo instead because they can't replicate it.
- **Deliberately fewer features than ClassDojo**: no parent accounts, no behaviour taxonomy, no setup. The things people complain about are the things we leave out. That's a position, not a limitation.
- **The name is Class? Yes!**, the call-and-response every teacher already uses to get the room: the teacher calls, the whole class answers. It is a whole-class moment, which is what the app is for. Written ClassYes wherever punctuation can't go (web address, app stores, search); classyes.app is the address to buy, and classyes.com is a premium domain worth buying only once teachers are paying. Names with "jar" were ruled out because ClassJar, Jar of Stars and Classroom Screen's behaviour jar already exist; Class Go, Class Ready, Ready Class and Ready to Learn all clash with products or programmes that exist. The phrase is too common to trademark on its own.
- **The logo is drawn in code, not a picture.** Two chalk speech bubbles, "Class?" in white and "Yes!" answering in yellow (`ClassYesLogo.tsx`), redrawn from a Gemini sketch. As an SVG it stays sharp on any board, and the words are live Andika, so the `a` is the one the students write. It is the splash screen's headline, with "Welcome, Teacher!" moved under it as a smaller line. The 2+2=4 doodle moved left to make room, because the taller headline pushed the button down on top of it. It moved again when Sign in with Google widened the row under the classes: it is up the left edge now, between the sun and the cat, and the chalk star sits between the dog and the house. **A splash taller than the screen is scaled down as a whole to fit**, rather than squeezed: five classes with Sign in on a 1024x640 laptop needed it, and five classes alone was already cut off top and bottom there. Screens it fits are untouched. **The bunting hangs over the top of the logo, on purpose** (2026-10-04): it was made a third shallower to clear the logo and put back the same day. The teacher likes it big, overlapping the logo the way real bunting hangs; shallow, it looked small and the board out of proportion. Don't shrink it again. **A doodle never sits on the words or a card**: they are placed by hand at the edges, but every new arrangement in the middle (one class or five, signed in or not, a short laptop or a big board) put one on top of something - the grapes on "...every computer", the cat and dog under the cards. The splash now hides any doodle that would touch the logo, a line of words, a class card or a button, measured before anything is painted and again when the screen or the fonts change.
- **The mascot is the splash screen's chalkboard, cheering**: wood frame, green board, a chalk smile with its eyes shut, both arms thrown up for the "Yes!" and a few stars. The artwork is the teacher's own, made in Gemini and traced to SVG in Canva. The whole character is the browser-tab icon (`public/favicon.svg`); the close-up tile (one arm, the frame's corner) is the home-screen icon (`apple-touch-icon.png`), on its own green because iOS fills see-through corners with black. A simpler redraw was made for tab size first and replaced by his: at 32px his reads fine, and at 16px any tab icon is a coloured square with a face. The speech bubbles stay as the name on the splash; the mascot is the face of the app where there is no room for words.
- Because it's designed to share the screen rather than own it, **coexisting with the lesson app is the core premise**, not a nice-to-have.

- **Treasure Quest: keys, five chests and a prize wheel** (2026-10-07, from the teacher's lessons; in a preview, "Treasure Quest Preview", a Claude artifact; nothing built yet). **Why**: reaching the goal lands flat unless the teacher has a real reward ready - the chest on the board is empty. The app can't supply the reward, and shouldn't try (no hatching or decorated avatars: they reward individuals, invite comparison, are ClassDojo's ground, and kids tire of in-app prizes faster than real ones). What it can do is give the goal a longer arc, build the suspense, and make the teacher's real reward easy and visible. **The shape agreed in talk**:
  - **Each goal filled wins a key**, which waits at the end of the meter's track; the chest sits just past it, with a little key for each lock. A key is a medium moment (it flies into the chest, a click, "You got a key!"), so **the big celebration moves to the chest opening**, rarer and so bigger - a change to the "goal is the only big celebration" rule, made on purpose.
  - **Five chests a quest, needing 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 keys** (Wood, Bronze, Silver, Gold, Mega). At about one key a week the Mega chest opens around week 15, so a quest is a term; then a new quest starts at chest 1 (not Mega forever, which would be a big party every five weeks). The goal size is the pace knob, per class: the teacher sees his ELA class 11 times a week, Math & Science 5 and Arts & Crafts 4, so ELA's goal will be two or three times Arts & Crafts'. The app could say how long the last key took.
  - **A wheel rises out of the opened chest** (the teacher's idea): eight slices, **question marks while it spins, the big prizes' slices gold**, so the class can root for gold without knowing which prize is where; only the slice it lands on turns over, then the prize fills the screen until tapped. **Spin is a tap, never a swipe**; **Pick a Spinner** chooses a student, or the teacher taps Spin.
  - **One prize list, each prize small, medium or big**; each chest's wheel is built from it with better odds as the chests go up (Wood 6 small, 2 medium; Bronze 5, 3; Silver 4, 3, 1 gold; Gold 2, 4, 2 gold; Mega 4 medium, 4 gold). **No empty slices, and the Mega wheel has no small prizes.** The starting list is the teacher's own rewards: drawing, reading, a game, music (small); a 10-minute video, the class picks the game, sitting with a friend (medium); the real treasure box, a toy party, candy (big); the library trip comes back when the library reopens. The prize is won on the spot; the teacher says when it happens.
  - **Our Quest**: a tap on the meter's chest slides the quest over the board - the chests opened so far with what was inside (class memories), the one now with its keys, and the Mega chest at the end. **It is drawn on an old treasure map** (2026-10-07, the teacher's comments on the preview): parchment with a faint ink grid, a compass, hills, an island and waves kept to the corners, and a red X under the Mega chest; **the chests zigzag and a dotted trail winds between them**: Wood low, Bronze high, Silver low, Gold high and the Mega chest in the middle at the end, the trail curving from one to the next and stopping short of each, so it never runs over a chest (the first straight dotted line ran behind them all, and footprints in the gaps were tried next and didn't look good), dark ink for the way walked and faint ahead; the zigzag is a share of the map's height, so a short board gets a gentler one, and the doodles sit in the room it leaves, above the low chests and below the high ones; **no line under the title**, since the pictures say how far the class has come, and the title says "Our Quest 2" from the second quest on; and **it never scrolls**: the chests, labels and footprints shrink to fit, and a long prize name wraps.

## Settled mechanics

- **The app never scrolls.** Not the board, not a screen that slides over it, not the side panel. Everything is laid out to fit the viewport, and when there is more than fits, the layout adapts (fewer rows, smaller chips) rather than the page growing. A modal may scroll its *body*, but the modal itself always fits — it is capped at 90% of the viewport with a scrolling body, so its title and edges never leave the screen. This is a rule, not a preference: a teacher at a board never scrolls to find a control.

- **Screen sizes and devices: it fits any screen from 1024x500 up, with no setting changed** (2026-10-02, the teacher's rule: "it will be used on various smartboards and screens, and must need no tweaking of Windows or browser settings"). The teacher's own board is a 4K panel with Windows at 300%, which, after Chrome's tabs, bookmarks bar and the taskbar, gives the page **1280x559** - shorter than anything it had been checked at, and the side panel ran off the bottom: by 106px with the timer controls open, 72px with the class list open, 8px on the flip cards. Turning Chrome's zoom or Windows' scaling down would have fixed his board and no one else's, so the app fits itself instead. **Nothing opens by pushing the side panel down**: the class list opens over the panel like a menu (a tap anywhere else closes it), and the timer controls are a drawer that **slides out from under the clock** and over Pickers & Points, in the timer's colour, while the time is being set; a tap on the timer slides it back, as before. The drawer's window starts tucked under the clock's rounded bottom and clips what is in it, so the controls come out from beneath the clock; it is one CSS slide the graphics chip runs. The first version faded in over the clock for a moment and then popped down, and on the board it looked broken (2026-10-03). **The side panel tightens itself to its height** (`hooks/useFitToHeight.ts`): drawn, measured, and if it overflows, one step tighter, before anything is painted - the gaps first, then the buttons' padding, then the dial, then the Pick All row. Text never shrinks for it, and a screen it fitted is drawn exactly as before. It measures again when something adds a line (a selection, the flip cards, the Saved mark), when the window is resized and when the fonts arrive. **Checked** at 1024x640, 1280x800 and 1920x1080 as before, and at the room real screens leave a page: 1024x500 (the floor), 1280x559 (his board), 1366x620, 1440x780, 1536x700 (1080p at 125%, common on laptops), 1920x940 and 2560x1300 - in every theme, both timer faces, with the timer controls and the class list open, a student selected and the flip cards up. The walkthrough's "nothing scrolls" checks the same list. The two old exceptions at 1024x640 (the timer controls with a student selected) are gone. **Since the side rail** (2026-10-10) the rail fits itself the same way (its gaps, then its icons), the timer floats over the board instead of opening over the panel, and the class list drops over the board from the bar; the walkthrough's "nothing scrolls" also checks the timer is wholly on the screen, big, with its choices, and put away. **It is not designed for phones or tablets**: they can technically open it in any browser (iOS Safari, Android Chrome, etc.), but the UI breaks—text is tiny, buttons are too small, and layouts overflow. A phone-first responsive redesign would be a major rebuild. A **phone UI is planned separately** (see "Phone as a remote control" in Wish list) for admin tasks and in-class shortcuts, not the full board. The app's core product is the smartboard experience; the phone extension is convenience and control from elsewhere in the room.

- **A long press opens no menu** (2026-10-06, the teacher). On a Windows touch board a finger held still is a right-click, so a child holding a desk, or the teacher holding +1 in the floating window, got Chrome's menu (Back, Reload, Print...) over the lesson. Other apps keep that menu out of their own area, and so does this one: anywhere in the app - the board, the windows, the splash and the floating window - a long press or a right-click does nothing (`lib/noLongPressMenu.ts`). Text boxes keep it, for pasting a name, and so do links, for copying one. It can't reach the floating window's title bar, which isn't the app's (Floating class goal, "Moving it by touch").

- **Smooth on the board: what moves, and how.** The teacher found the whole app laggy and choppy, at school and at home on any connection. It was never the internet: once the app is open nothing waits on it (each picture and sound is fetched the first time it's used). It was how hard the app made the device work, found by driving the production build at a board's speed - processor slowed 4x, 4K-like pixel density - and counting frames. Two causes. **Frosted glass**: the side panel, the goal bar, the flip-card and group toolbars, the dialog dim and the status picker blurred what was behind them, and a browser redoes every blur on every frame anything on screen moves. On a 4K board that halved the frame rate, and the dial timer, moving every frame inside the blurred panel, ran at 35. They are the same see-through colours without the blur; the dialog dim went from 40% to 50% and the status picker's to 60% so they still stand apart from the board. The only blur left is Delete Class's small safety cover, whose job is to hide the button under it. **Animations run by the main processor**: framer-motion moved things frame by frame in JavaScript - nine sparkles per point, stars swirling round a picked desk, thirty cards dealt, thirty name chips dealt, a spring on every button press, the flip clock's leaves every second - and made the page be measured on every render for a side-panel glide used once a term. **What went**, with the teacher asking for anything not really needed to go: the stars round a picked student or row (the picked desk now has a still amber outline, the pickers' colour everywhere else, and an inked black one in Comic Book, where amber disappears into the yellow ground), the +1 sparkles on the goal bar and, once it was live, the "+1" that rose off the coin on every point, and the one that rose off the floating window's +1 button (the count popping and the coin moving already say it), the brightness filter on a flickering desk or group card, and the side panel's glide when it changes sides. **What stayed, moved to CSS or `element.animate`**, which the graphics chip runs by itself, and looks the same: the goal bar's coin, fill, count pop and chest rattle; every button's press; the flip clock's leaves; the flip-card deal, turn, lift and fade; and the group deal, where each chip remembers where it was drawn and glides home from there - one small glide for gathering, dealing, moving a name and the chips closing up behind it. The dial redraws only as often as its edge moves, every half degree (about 12 times a second for a 1-minute timer, once a second for 20 minutes), not 60 times a second. And the windows no longer build their contents while closed: Class Settings built the roster and a month of attendance on every desk tap. Measured at 4K-like density with the processor slowed 4x, frames per second before → after: dial timer 35 → 57, flipping cards 30 → 52, +1 39 → 56, picking a student 46 → 58, dealing groups 33 → 50, opening flip cards 38 → 50, tapping desks 50 → 56. What is left is a one-off pause as the flip-card and group screens are first built. The rules this leaves are in CLAUDE.md; `scripts/frame-check.mjs` repeats the measurement.

- **Setting up a class quickly** (2026-10-05, from lesson one; Next up has the story). **Paste a list**: a list copied from Excel or a Google Sheet comes in with no file saved. Tabs (Excel and Sheets), commas (a CSV's text) and one name a line all work. **Excel files as they are**: the file window shows Excel files as well as CSV (it showed only CSV, so Windows hid the teacher's file), and an .xlsx goes through the same column finding. The old .xls isn't read: choosing one says to save it as .xlsx or paste the names, and a file that can't be read says to paste instead. **An empty class says how**, where the roster will be: "Copy your student list from Excel and paste it here.", with **Paste a List** and **Choose a File**, and a line that Boy or Girl can wait and a student who joins later is added above. Import is now the same short menu signed in or out; signed out it used to be Import CSV alone. **Tested** with lists as Excel and Sheets copy them, with Excel files made by a spreadsheet library (a heading row; no heading, starting at B3, with 男/女), an old .xls and a broken file, at 1024x500 up (`scripts/walkthrough.mjs`, "excel and a pasted list", with `scripts/fixtures/roster.xlsx`).
- **Typeface**: Andika, app-wide. Chosen for its single-storey lowercase `a`, which is how the students are taught to write it; Comic Sans was rejected. Lexend was a close second. `lib/fitText.ts` measures in Andika because it picks one name size for a whole class — measuring in the wrong face would size every desk wrong.
- **Desk layout**: rounded top, square bottom. **An empty desk is just the faded tile**: the word "Empty" on every one was one more word on a board already full of names. The star chip sits top-right over the avatar's empty corner (only with stars on the desks first; see "Two ways of running points"), leaving the whole bottom row to the name. **The homeroom number shows after the name, small and quiet, and only where two students share a name** (below). One name size per class, computed from the longest name, so no student's desk reads smaller than the rest. **Names use their own colour, `--desk-name`, softer than the theme's text colour.** On a smartboard the names were the loudest thing on screen — big, bold and near-black at about 14:1 contrast, against 6:1 for the app's own button text — and read as glaring. Size was not the problem: a smaller name was still harsh, and a lighter weight is a big jump because Andika has only regular and bold, and thin strokes are the first thing a projector washes out. So the names stay bold and full size, and Light and Elementary each use a dark tinted from their own palette at about 9:1, well clear of the 7:1 readability bar. Dark (white on dark) and Comic Book (ink on purpose) keep their card text colour, and Chalkboard writes them in chalk white on a desk a shade deeper than the board, also about 9:1.
- **Themes**: five, chosen in Class Settings → Class — Light, Dark, Chalkboard, Elementary (the default until a teacher picks otherwise; only a pick in the picker is remembered, never the OS preference) and Comic Book. School Colors, built from the Yuteh crest, was removed at the teacher's request. Sky Blue made way for Chalkboard, in its slot in the picker: Light, Sky Blue and Elementary were all light and blue, so it was the one most like the others. A teacher who had a removed theme picked lands on Elementary. Three of the five are pure CSS-variable swaps and can stay that way. **Comic Book is the exception and is allowed to be**: what makes a page look printed is ink weight, a halftone lattice and hard shadows, none of which is a colour. So it carries a structural block in `index.css` alongside its tokens, under two rules that keep that from spreading. It only reaches for hooks that already exist — `data-slot` on the shadcn primitives, plus `data-ink` on the three app containers that had none (canvas, panel, desk) — and it never touches anything that affects layout: every outline is a `box-shadow` ring rather than a border, so switching themes cannot reflow the board. Bangers letters the chrome; student names stay in Andika, because an all-caps display face is the wrong thing for a roster of real names read across a room. Colour varies per desk through a rotating five, carried in a caption bar across the panel's top rather than a wash or a centred glow — both of those put saturated colour behind the student's name. Texture alternates between a halftone lattice and diagonal speed ruling, so neighbouring panels aren't printed the same way. **Chalkboard is the second exception, under the same two rules.** It came from the teacher's picture of a green classroom board with coloured chalk doodles and a wooden ledge. The board is chalkboard green with chalk dust (the splash screen's grain) and a wooden frame, drawn as an inset shadow inside the canvas's own padding so nothing moves. Desks are slate tiles a shade deeper than the board, outlined in soft white chalk: the side panel buttons' chalk, drawn a little heavier, with the homeroom number in the same chalk a step quieter than the name. The first version gave each desk one of five chalk colours on a diagonal, like Comic Book's caption bars, and on a real board thirty coloured outlines were a distraction from the names. It is a dark theme underneath, so the `dark:` variant covers it as well as Dark. The splash screen's chalk face (Cabin Sketch) letters the class name, panel headings and dialog titles; names stay in Andika for the same reason as Comic Book. Switched-on buttons are yellow chalk, dialogs get the wooden frame, and the flip deck and group cards get the dust through `data-ink="board"`. Flip card backs and group colours stay as they are in every theme.

- **Group Activity**: one button on the side panel, one modal, one screen. The modal is all buttons, no typing: how many groups (2–6), groups of (pairs, 3s, 4s, 5s), or split by boys/girls or rows, each captioned with what it makes of *this* class today ("7–8 each", "15 groups") and greyed out with the reason when it can't. Counts and sizes come from where the students sit (below). Tapping one deals: the chips gather into a stack in the middle of the board and are dealt out one at a time into colour-banded cards, with the side panel's pickers and +/- standing down the way they do for Swap Seats — the timer stays live. Building groups by hand is deliberately not a mode (thirty students is sixty taps); fix-ups are tap a chip, then tap the card it belongs in — anywhere on the card, chips included. Dragging was built and taken out: on a touch board it fights with scrolling, and one way to do a thing is easier to teach than two. Chips are name and homeroom number only, no avatars, **one size for the whole class regardless of how many groups there are**, all the same width (set by the longest name, like the desks' one name size), so they line up in rows; a name past the cap shrinks inside its chip. Cards fit their contents and every card in a deal shares the tallest one's height, centred on the board, so empty space is around the cards rather than inside them. **The board never scrolls**: a planner knows every card's dimensions, so it picks the column count that fits at full-size chips, then tightens the card's chrome (three densities: padding, light size and button size give way in that order), and only when nothing fits does it shrink the chips a step (all of them, so one size for the class holds) and try again — names are what has to be read from the back of the room, so they are the last thing to give. The chrome is computed from the classes, not measured, since the plan decides which density renders; the frame is a real border, since a ring outside the card was clipped wherever it met the board's edge. Leftovers spread into existing groups rather than forming a group of one. Each card carries its own score, with the same +/- as the side panel. **Exit Group Activity** is the one way out: with nothing scored it just closes; with points on the board it asks whether they go onto the class goal now or wait with the groups — handing the points out is not a side effect of leaving a screen. **Group points go to the class goal** (2026-10-03, the teacher: points go to the whole class). A group's points build on its card during the activity, and when they go out each group point becomes one class point on the goal meter. The Points Go To choice, Each Student or Class Goal, was taken out of the Group Activity window, and with it stars for each member. A class with its goal switched off has it switched on when the points go out (with its last goal, or 50), so they land where the class can see them, as choosing Class Goal used to do. Groups survive exit, so "Continue with Last Groups" picks the same teams up tomorrow, scores included if they were kept; anyone no longer seated drops out on the way; a shuffle keeps each card's identity by position so only the chips move. Colours are fixed across themes (they *are* the group's identity); everything else follows the theme, like Flip Cards. **No group is red, amber, orange, green or grey**, because those are the statuses and the status is what the room scans for: the set used to include the exact red of Help and green of Done, so Group 1 always wore a red band while working happily, and "look for the red card" stopped working. Twelve colours now (blue, pink, teal, violet, brown and charcoal first, so the usual two to six groups are six different families); pairs of a full 35 make 17 groups, so the last few repeat. The Girls group went from rose, which read as Help, to pink. Groups saved in an old colour are recoloured by position when they come back. Names are Group 1, 2, 3 (or Boys and Girls) and can't be changed, so nothing in Group Activity asks anyone to type.
  - **Groups are made from where the students sit** (2026-10-05, the teacher's call: Split by Rows already kept the rows together, and pairs didn't). Carrying chairs across the room is the nightmare a group activity starts with, so "How Many Groups?" and "Or Groups Of…" are worked out from the room's own plan (`lib/seatGroups.ts`), and **Shuffle is the way to mix them**. On six across and five deep: **pairs** are the two desks side by side, **threes** the three side by side, **fours** two side by side and the two behind them (seven groups: the front line, which a class leaves short since it fills from the back, makes the last group, its leftover desk joining the square behind it), **fives** each row front to back. **How many groups** cuts the room into that many patches: six are the rows, three two rows each, two the halves, five the lines across, four the corners, always as even as they can be. Pairs and Rows of 3 group the desks pushed together; at tables a group of four or five is a table, a pair two side by side at one. The shape is chosen from the room's desks, not from who is in today, so one absence doesn't change how everyone else sits. **Gaps**: a group with everyone in it stays exactly as it sits, so one absence doesn't ripple across the room; one missing a single student stays as it is (a square of three); at a table, a leftover desk joins its own table; **a student whose partner is away pairs with the nearest other student left on their own, however far** (the teacher's rule), and a last one on their own joins the nearest group; other short bits join a short bit close by, and nobody is ever a group of one. "How many groups" walks the room in one unbroken snake, strip by strip, and cuts the walk into even shares, so each group is one patch even when absences or a short front line move the cut; anyone the cut leaves cut off from their group trades places with a neighbour. **Making groups again with the same choice gives the same groups**: Shuffle deals everyone at random into the same number of cards, the one way to mix a class up (it used to re-deal the same choice at random, and did nothing for Split by Rows). The captions under the buttons count what the seats will actually make. Checked: the teacher's shapes exactly on a full board and on 23 to 35 students, every layout, absences, and 10,000 random classes with about one in eight away: nobody missing, doubled or alone, how many groups always even, and 1.3% of groups with someone more than one empty desk from every groupmate, most of them the two left alone pairing up.
  - **Continue with Last Groups says how they were made**, in the buttons' own words: "Pairs · 15 groups", "Groups of 4 · 7 groups", "4 groups", "Boys / Girls", "Rows · 6 groups" (`groupsMadeBy`, kept with the class; groups saved before it say just "15 groups"). A row of coloured dots, one per card and at most eight, used to sit there and said nothing a teacher could choose by (2026-10-05, the teacher was confused by them). Because the class remembers how its groups were made, **Shuffle works after Continue** too; it used to grey out with "Tap New Groups to shuffle".
  - **Status.** Groups are for projects as much as games, so every card carries one of four states: Working, Help, Ready, Done. Ready and Done are deliberately different — Ready means a group has stopped and is waiting for the teacher, Done means there is nothing left to do. **The status colours the card, it is not a widget on the card.** The body and the points row both take a **strong** wash in the status colour and the band and frame keep the group's own colour, so scanning the room is "look for the red card", which is the red, amber and green cups on a group's table. The wash is deliberately heavy: the first attempt used a 16% tint and from the back of a room it was indistinguishable from the next card. Name chips sit on the plain card colour so they stay crisp on top of it. The small status chip in the band wears the status colour too, ringed in the band's own text colour so it still separates when a red group is the one asking for help — a faded chip said nothing at a distance and the chip and the body should say the same thing. Working has no wash at all: if every card were tinted the board would go one flat colour and a single red card would stop standing out. Help pulses a soft ring, and it is the only status that moves, because it is the only one asking for you now. Icons are Pencil, question mark, thumbs up and tick, chosen to be read without English — a raised hand turns to mush at small sizes and a flag has to be taught. Help, Ready and Done chime, switchable in the Group Activity modal. Statuses survive Exit and Continue and reset with a new deal.
  - **Group names cannot be edited.** They are Group 1, 2, 3 or Boys and Girls, and that is enough — a teacher who wants "Red Team" can say it. Renaming existed briefly and its field stretched from the name to the status chip, which mostly served as a way for a student to open a dialog by accident. Removing it removed the accident and a whole modal.
    - **The first version was a strip of four lights on the card and it was wrong twice over.** The lit light expanded in place, so its position moved from card to card and a board of fifteen never lined up; and the strip had a fixed 200px minimum the layout planner never reserved, so at fifteen groups it was clipped at both ends. Cards were optimised one at a time and never looked at fifteen side by side.
  - **Choosing a status is a panel low on the screen, not buttons on the card.** A card in the top row of a smartboard is two metres up and a small card would mean small buttons, which is worst for the shortest student. So the board dims, the card flies down to a fixed spot and four large buttons sit beneath it in the bottom third. One row, left to right, in the order work goes: a two by two grid leaves a child guessing whether to read across or down. Every button wears its status colour, so the code is taught each time. **No close button and no instructions**: the group's current status is one of the four and is shown as already chosen, so a child who opened it by accident taps the lit one and nothing happens. Tapping outside closes it too. The panel is solid over a board dimmed by 60% rather than frosted — the deep dim is what makes it feel lifted, and two translucent layers read as muddy on a projector that has already eaten the contrast. It was a blur, which a 4K board redid on every frame anything moved behind it (see Smooth on the board).
  - **The points row is sized by the card's width, not by the density.** Density is chosen to make cards fit by *height*, so a board that went to eight columns could leave a card half as wide carrying a row built for a wide one — which is how the plus and minus ended up clipped at the card's edges. Widths and icon sizes now come from the card's width while heights still come from the density, so the planner's arithmetic stays true.
  - **Picking, during a group activity, reuses the side panel's two picker buttons.** Pick Row becomes **Pick Group** while the cards are up — a row and a group are both "a set of students", so the button keeps its meaning and only what counts as a set changes — and Pick Student flashes across the name chips instead of the desks. The pickers are a family and a teacher already looks in that box for them; putting one in the group toolbar would split them across two places and add a sixth control to a row that wraps. They were greyed out before because the desk picker lights up desks, which are behind the cards and so invisible here, so `useGroupPicker` is a separate hook at the same pace and with the same sounds, pointed at the cards. The winner is ringed in amber, the one colour that is never a group's and never a status. It honours the same Allow Repeats setting, tracked per activity and reset on a new deal, and a landed pick clears on a tap anywhere. **A picked group keeps Pick Student inside it**, the way a picked row does on the desks (2026-10-03, the teacher's call): once Pick Group lands, Pick Student reads **Pick from This Group** and draws only from that group, whose card keeps its amber ring while the others stay faded. A tap on the board lets the group go and the button reads Pick Student again; so does a new deal, and so does the group running out of students who haven't had a turn (with Allow Repeats off), one pick before it would stop meaning it, as Pick from This Row does.
    - Two framer-motion traps here, both worth remembering: it will not clear an inline `box-shadow` it has set, so the winner's ring had to be a class or every past winner stayed ringed; and on an element with a `layoutId` the shared-layout pass writes an inline `opacity` that no utility class can beat, so a dimmed chip's opacity has to go through `animate`. (The chips no longer use framer at all - see Smooth on the board - but the traps stand for anything that still does.)
  - **The riffle belongs to the Shuffle button.** A first deal played it too, so making groups sounded like two sounds landing on top of each other; there is nothing to shuffle when the cards are being made for the first time.
  - **Reset Points** sits in the group toolbar, greyed until something has been scored, behind a confirm. It zeroes the scores and leaves the groups alone, for running the same teams through a second round.
  - **Settings and Swap Seats stand down during a group activity.** Both rearrange the class underneath a running activity, and Swap Seats acts on desks that are behind the cards. Swap Seats is out whenever the cards are up, flip cards included (2026-10-03: it acts on the desks, so it is only for the seating chart); Settings goes out when the board is locked, which is when students are the ones standing at it.
  - **Lock** is for students coming up to the board. It freezes what would destroy work: moving a name, New Groups, Shuffle, Exit and the side panel's button. **Points and status stay live**, because awarding a point for being on task mid activity is normal and unlocking to do it would be backwards. It is a courtesy lock, not a PIN.
